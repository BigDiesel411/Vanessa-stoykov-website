Should I Stay or Should I Sell? The Property Decision That Gets Harder in Your 50s

By Vanessa Stoykov

In midlife, property decisions carry history. The family home may represent children growing up, a marriage, years of renovations and the neighbourhood that knows you. It may also carry a large mortgage, rising maintenance and rooms no longer used — sometimes both of those things sitting inside the same set of walls at once.

That is why 'just downsize' can feel glib. You are not only comparing sale prices. You are considering identity, community, security and the shape of the next chapter, and none of those weigh as neatly as a bank valuation does.

Start with the future, not the floor plan

Imagine ordinary life five and ten years from now. How much space will you use? Where will family live? How important are transport, stairs, health services and a support network? Would a move free time and money, or take you away from what makes life work day to day?

A smaller home is not automatically cheaper once transaction costs, strata fees, renovations and location are considered. Use realistic figures rather than assumptions inherited from a friend's very different experience.

IN PRACTICE

One widow held onto her four-bedroom family home for nearly a decade after her children moved out, convinced downsizing meant giving something up. When she finally sold and moved into a smaller two-bedroom apartment closer to her sister, she was surprised to find the grief she'd anticipated never really arrived — what she felt instead was relief at no longer maintaining rooms she hadn't entered in years, and the extra cash freed up a level of financial breathing room she hadn't had in a long time.

Measure pressure as well as equity

A valuable home can make you look wealthy while leaving very little cash flow. Consider repayments, rates, repairs, insurance and the amount of work required to keep the property functioning — the paper wealth and the lived experience of the household are not always the same thing.

On the other hand, selling too early or buying again impulsively can create new costs. Compare several scenarios: stay and adjust, sell and rent, downsize nearby, relocate or make the current home suitable for longer.

Give the decision time

Divorce, bereavement, redundancy and children leaving home can create urgency to change everything. Unless circumstances force a quick decision, allow the emotional dust to settle before locking in a major, hard-to-reverse move.

Get independent advice on the financial, tax, legal and retirement implications. Then ask which option gives you the best balance of security, flexibility and belonging — not simply the highest number on paper.

A conversation worth having

If this issue is part of your life, begin with questions that create clarity rather than blame:

  • What will daily life here look like in ten years?
  • How much does the home truly cost each year?
  • What would I gain and lose by moving?
  • Am I deciding from pressure, grief or a clear plan?

The right home for the first half of your life may not be the right one for the next. Changing it is not erasing your history. It can be a deliberate way of making room for what comes next.

Discuss major property decisions with appropriately qualified financial, legal, tax and lending professionals.
