Why Property FOMO Can Be an Expensive Emotion

By Vanessa Stoykov

Property fear of missing out has a particular intensity. Prices rise, friends buy, auctions move quickly and every delay feels like permanent failure. The mind jumps from 'I have not bought yet' to 'I will never be able to buy,' and that leap happens far faster than the actual market usually moves.

Under that pressure, people stop evaluating a property and start trying to escape a feeling. That is when compromises multiply and limits begin to move, often without the buyer even noticing how far they've shifted from where they started.

Urgency narrows your vision

FOMO encourages buyers to treat all action as progress. They overlook building issues, accept unsuitable locations, waive sensible checks or borrow to the edge of their capacity because doing nothing feels unbearable in the moment.

The danger is not only overpaying. It is spending years servicing a decision that never matched the life you wanted, made in a headspace that had very little to do with the actual property in front of you.

IN PRACTICE

After watching three auctions go well above their budget, one couple finally "won" a fourth by bidding $60,000 over their original limit — relief flooding in the moment the hammer fell. It was only weeks later, once the adrenaline wore off, that they properly registered the commute, the renovation costs they'd skipped assessing, and the repayment figure they'd agreed to under pressure. They later said the auction room had felt more like a competition to win than a decision about where they'd actually live.

Replace headlines with personal numbers

Markets are broad; your position is specific. Work from your deposit, reliable income, buffers, future plans and tolerance for repayment pressure. Model what happens if rates, employment or family circumstances change — before you're standing in a room with a paddle in your hand.

Create non-negotiables before inspections begin. Include the maximum total cost, required checks, acceptable locations and the financial buffer that must remain after settlement.

Waiting can be an active decision

Waiting is not always wise, but neither is it always failure. A period of saving, reducing debt, improving income or learning a market can strengthen your position rather than simply delaying the inevitable.

If you decide to buy, let it be because the property and commitment work — not because someone else's purchase made you panic. There will always be another headline. You only have to live with your own loan.

A conversation worth having

If this issue is part of your life, begin with questions that create clarity rather than blame:

  • What fear is creating this deadline?
  • Which checks will I never waive?
  • What is my comfortable limit, not just approval limit?
  • Would I want this property if nobody knew I bought it?

FOMO tells you that the most dangerous choice is missing out. In property, the more expensive danger can be getting in at any cost.

A no-obligation conversation with a lending professional can help you understand your position before you start bidding.
