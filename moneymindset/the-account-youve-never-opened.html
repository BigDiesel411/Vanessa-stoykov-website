The Account You've Never Opened

By Conrad Francis

Every year the same message arrives. A letter, an email, a notification that sits on the home screen for a day and then doesn't. It's about her superannuation. And every year it gets the same treatment, a glance, a genuine intention to look at it properly, and then a folder where things go to be dealt with later.

Call her Ellie. She's in her early forties, she's had four employers, and she's a composite rather than a real person, a stand-in for a pattern that turns up in a lot of Australian households. Ellie is not careless with money. She knows roughly what she spends on groceries in a week. She'd spot an odd charge on her card within a day. But her super, quite possibly the second-largest asset she will ever own, is a number she couldn't tell you within a wide margin, sitting somewhere she'd have to search her inbox to name.

Ask her why and she won't say she doesn't care. She'll say she's been meaning to. She'll say it's complicated. She'll say she'll get to it when things settle down.

The gap between what people intend to do about their super and what they actually do is the part worth looking at. And it is almost never an information problem.

The obvious explanation is that superannuation is confusing. The rules change, the language is technical, the annual statement is dense. All fair. But complexity on its own doesn't explain the behaviour, because Ellie deals with complicated things constantly. She has navigated a mortgage application, a school enrolment, an insurance claim. None of those are simple, and none of them sat in a folder for a decade.

What makes super different is that nothing happens when you ignore it. It is money you can't reach, managed by someone else, on behalf of a version of you who doesn't exist yet. There's no bounced payment, no phone call, no consequence that lands this week. Every other part of a household budget gives feedback. Super gives silence, and silence is very easy to mistake for fine.

Then there's the second thing, which people rarely say out loud, opening the statement makes it real. As long as the number is unknown, it can still be whatever you hope it is. Look at it, and you've committed to having a view about it. Not knowing has a strange comfort to it. That isn't stupidity, it's a very human piece of self-protection, and naming it does more good than another explainer on the rules.

So the useful move isn't to learn more. It's to look at what's stopping the looking. Three questions, worth sitting with honestly before touching anything.

What am I actually worried I'll find? Most people, pressed, will admit the fear isn't confusion, it's that they're behind. Worth knowing whether that's what you're avoiding, because “I'm behind” and “I don't understand this” are entirely different problems with entirely different answers.

How many accounts do I have, and where are they? Not what's in them. Just the number of accounts and where they live. People who have changed jobs a few times often can't answer this, and it's the smallest possible first step, pure fact-finding, with no decision attached to it.

Whose choices am I currently living with? Most super arrangements were set by a form filled in years ago, at a job someone has since left, by a person they no longer entirely are. That isn't a mistake. But there's a real difference between a decision you made and a default you inherited, and it's worth knowing which one you're standing in.

Notice what none of those questions ask you to do. They don't ask you to change anything, choose anything, or understand anything technical. They ask you to know where you stand. Almost everyone skips that step and jumps straight to what they should do, which is exactly why the folder stays shut. You can't make a good decision about a situation you've never described.

Ellie's account isn't unopened because superannuation is hard. It's unopened because opening it would make it hers, and there's a small, understandable part of her that would rather it stayed someone else's problem for another year.

It won't. It's hers either way. The only real question is whether she meets it now, while there's still room to do something with what she finds, or later, when the choices have narrowed to whatever is left.
