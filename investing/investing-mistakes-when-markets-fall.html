The Investing Mistakes We Make When Markets Fall

By Vanessa Stoykov

When markets rise, risk can feel theoretical. When balances fall, the loss feels personal and immediate. Headlines become urgent, confident predictions appear everywhere and doing something feels safer than waiting. This is when long-term plans are often most vulnerable to short-term fear.

This is where investing becomes less about finding a fashionable answer and more about making a decision that fits an actual human life. A market fall does not create your investing emotions. It reveals them. The numbers matter, but so do your obligations, confidence, time frame and ability to stay with the decision when life becomes complicated.

Selling to escape the feeling

A fall on a statement is painful, but selling turns the current value into an actual exit. Sometimes a sale is appropriate, but panic alone is not a strategy. Ask whether your goal, time frame or circumstances changed, or whether only the price and your emotional state changed.

BREAKOUT BOX 1 — THE SIMPLE EXPLANATION
  • Market falls are a normal possibility, not a personal failure.
  • Selling removes exposure to both further falls and potential recovery.
  • Re-entering later requires another difficult decision.
  • An emotional plan is part of an investment plan.

Do not turn fear into prediction

Investors may sell because they plan to return when conditions feel safe. Markets can recover before confidence does, making re-entry just as difficult. Others chase whichever asset held up best in the last fall, assuming yesterday's winner will provide tomorrow's protection.

There is also an emotional layer that is easy to overlook. Investing can stir up fear of getting it wrong, embarrassment about what you do not know and pressure to catch up with people who appear further ahead. Those feelings are real, but they should not be allowed to decide what you buy, how much you commit or when you change direction.

Return to the plan

Review diversification, cash needs, contribution levels and risk. If a fall shows that the portfolio exceeds your genuine tolerance, seek advice and adjust deliberately rather than in the middle of panic. Reduce the volume of commentary if it encourages impulsive action. Your time frame should determine your attention span.

Bring the decision back to four practical lenses: the emotion you are experiencing, the implications for your everyday life, the financial consequences and the longer-term view. A choice can look attractive in isolation and still be wrong for the wider life it has to support.

Good investing behaviour is often unremarkable. It involves reading, asking questions, allowing for uncertainty, keeping records and reviewing decisions at sensible intervals. It rarely requires constant action. In fact, the pressure to do something immediately is often a reason to slow down and check what is driving the urgency.

If you decide to seek advice, arrive with your questions and expect clear explanations. Professional support should help you understand your choices; it should not require you to surrender your voice. You remain entitled to know what is being recommended, why it may fit, what it costs and what could go wrong.

One final point is worth remembering: an investment decision should never be viewed separately from the rest of your finances. Housing, debt, emergency savings, insurance, family responsibilities and future income all affect the uncertainty you can reasonably carry. The same investment may play a different role for two people because their lives are different. Comparison can provide information, but it cannot provide your answer.

BREAKOUT BOX 2 — BEFORE YOU ACT
  • Check whether your personal circumstances changed.
  • Protect near-term spending needs.
  • Review diversification and risk capacity.
  • Seek advice before making large irreversible changes.

A conversation worth having

Before making the next decision, ask yourself:

  • Has my goal changed?
  • Do I need this money soon?
  • Is the portfolio diversified?
  • Am I acting from evidence or seeking relief?

A sound investment plan should account for two unavoidable facts: markets will sometimes fall, and human beings will become frightened when they do. Courage is not pretending the fear is absent. It is creating enough space to decide from more than fear alone.

Seek appropriately qualified advice before making significant changes during volatile markets.
